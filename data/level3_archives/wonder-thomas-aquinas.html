II. Unsolved Enigmas and the Mythology of the Subject

The intellectual grandeur of Thomas Aquinas is matched only by the enigmatic dimensions that envelop his life and work, casting a shadowy intrigue that invites both admiration and profound inquiry. Central to this tapestry of enigma is the question of Aquinas's mystical experiences, which remain shrouded in both reverence and ambiguity. Aquinas, known for his rigorous intellectual discipline, is also reported to have undergone profound mystical experiences, particularly during the latter years of his life. According to hagiographic accounts, one such experience occurred while celebrating Mass in 1273 when he reportedly received a divine revelation that led him to declare his extensive body of work as "straw" in comparison to the ineffable truths he had glimpsed. This incident raises intriguing questions about the relationship between reason and mysticism in Aquinas's thought, a duality that has invited centuries of interpretation and speculation.

The mystical dimension of Aquinas's life is further accentuated by the enigmatic cessation of his writing. Following the aforementioned revelation, Aquinas ceased to write, leaving his seminal work, the "Summa Theologica," unfinished. This abrupt end is a source of profound mystery: Was it a response to divine instruction, a reflection of the ultimate limits of human reason, or perhaps an acknowledgment of the inadequacy of words to capture the divine? The theological implications of this cessation resonate deeply within the annals of scholastic thought, prompting a reevaluation of the boundaries between human knowledge and divine mystery.

Another enigma resides in the circumstances surrounding Aquinas's death. The cause of his sudden illness and demise has been the subject of speculation, with theories ranging from astrological influences, as was common in medieval interpretations, to more mundane explanations such as a bacterial infection. Yet, the mysterious nature of his death has contributed to a quasi-mythical narrative, intertwining Aquinas's mortality with the unfathomable mystery of divine providence.

The mythology of Thomas Aquinas is further enriched by the enduring legends of his posthumous influence and the miraculous nature of his relics. Reports of miracles attributed to Aquinas began soon after his death, consolidating his status as a saint and a figure of veneration. The preservation and veneration of his relics, particularly in the Dominican tradition, have added layers of sacred aura to his legacy, deepening the mystical allure that surrounds his persona. These relics, and the narratives they inspire, speak to the enduring power of Aquinas's spiritual and intellectual contributions, transcending the boundaries of time and space.

Moreover, the mythological aspect of Aquinas's legacy is not solely confined to the realm of theology and philosophy. His integration of Aristotelian thought into Christian doctrine has been seen as a legendary act of intellectual synthesis, a transformative moment that shaped the trajectory of Western thought. The mythologized narrative of Aquinas as the "Angelic Doctor" is one that underscores his role as a bridge between ancient wisdom and medieval scholasticism, a narrative that is as much about intellectual heroism as it is about theological innovation.

The enigma of Thomas Aquinas is also reflected in the diverse interpretations and appropriations of his work throughout history. From the Renaissance humanists who viewed him as a beacon of rational inquiry to the Counter-Reformation thinkers who saw him as a bulwark against Protestant challenges, Aquinas's legacy is a palimpsest of interpretations, each adding to the mythological dimensions of his influence. This multiplicity of interpretations underscores the complexity and depth of Aquinas's thought, inviting continuous exploration and reinterpretation.

In conclusion, the unsolved enigmas and the mythology surrounding Thomas Aquinas are integral to understanding his enduring impact on the tapestry of human knowledge. As we continue to delve into the depths of his genius, we are reminded that the legacy of Thomas Aquinas is not confined to the pages of history but lives on as a profound source of wonder and inspiration.

IV. Philosophy, Sacred Mathematics, and the Vision of the Universe

The intellectual odyssey of Thomas Aquinas unfolds not only within the hallowed halls of theological discourse but also in the labyrinthine corridors of philosophy and mathematics, where the sacred and the rational converge in a celestial dance. To comprehend Aquinas's vision of the universe, one must traverse the intertwining paths of Aristotelian logic, Platonic ideals, and Pythagorean numerology, each strand weaving a complex tapestry of cosmic understanding.

Aquinas was an ardent devotee of synthesis—a harmonizer of seemingly disparate philosophies. His philosophical inquiries were profoundly influenced by the works of Aristotle, whose empirical methods and logical structures laid the groundwork for Aquinas's own metaphysical explorations. Yet, Aquinas's genius lay in his ability to transcend mere Aristotelian thought, infusing it with the theological profundities of Christian doctrine. This synthesis is epitomized in his magnum opus, the Summa Theologica, where he articulates a universe governed by divine reason, a cosmos ordered by an intelligence that is both immanent and transcendent.

In exploring the sacred mathematics that underpins Aquinas's vision, one must recognize the medieval belief in numerology as a divine language. Numbers were not mere abstract entities but were imbued with spiritual significance, a reflection of the divine order. Aquinas, inspired by the Pythagorean tradition, viewed mathematics as a bridge between the earthly and the divine—a conduit through which the rational mind could approach the mysteries of God. His philosophical treatises suggest that the harmony of the universe is a reflection of a higher mathematical order, a cosmic symphony composed by the Creator.

The concept of ratio, for Aquinas, was pivotal. It represented the rational principle, the intrinsic order that governs all creation. He posited that the natural world, with its patterns and regularities, was a testament to this divine rationality. By engaging with sacred mathematics, Aquinas believed that one could glimpse the mind of God, understanding the universe as a manifestation of divine wisdom. His vision was one where faith and reason were not antagonistic but complementary, each illuminating the path to ultimate truth.

The universe, in Aquinas's philosophy, is a reflection of both ontological and epistemological realities. Ontologically, it acknowledges the presence of a hierarchical order—a Great Chain of Being—where every entity, from the simplest mineral to the most complex celestial being, has its place in the divine schema. This hierarchical vision affirms a universe that is purposeful and interconnected, a cosmos where every component plays a role in the grand design.

Epistemologically, Aquinas emphasizes the role of human reason as a tool to decipher this divine order. He advances the notion of analogia entis, the analogy of being, which suggests that while humans can never fully comprehend God, they can understand His creation through analogy. This concept is deeply mathematical in its essence, as it relies on proportionalities and relationships to bridge the gap between the finite and the infinite, the created and the Creator.

In his cosmological framework, Aquinas also engages with the Platonic idea of the anima mundi, the world soul, which posits that the universe is alive, imbued with a spiritual essence that animates all things. This aligns with his belief in the immanence of God, a divine presence that permeates every atom of the cosmos. For Aquinas, the physical universe is a sacrament—a visible sign of an invisible reality, a tangible expression of divine grace.

Moreover, Aquinas's philosophical musings extend to the concept of time and eternity. He distinguishes between the tempus, the temporal order experienced by humans, and aevum, the eternal present in which God exists. This duality reflects a universe that is both temporal and eternal, bound by the constraints of time yet eternally anchored in the divine presence. Aquinas's vision challenges us to perceive time not as a linear progression but as a cyclical, eternal dance, where the past, present, and future are enfolded into the divine now.

In conclusion, Thomas Aquinas's philosophy and sacred mathematics present a rich and intricate vision of the universe—a cosmos where the rational and the mystical are inextricably linked, where every mathematical equation is a note in the divine symphony. His legacy invites us to explore the universe not merely as a physical entity but as a spiritual reality, urging us to seek wisdom through both faith and reason. As we continue to unravel the mysteries of the cosmos, Aquinas's vision remains a beacon, guiding us toward a deeper understanding of the universe and our place within it.
